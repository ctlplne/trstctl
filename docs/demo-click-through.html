Before you start

Keep this guide in one tab and the demo in another. Plan on 35–50 minutes.

Demo address

http://127.0.0.1:18081

Demo user

demo-admin@trstctl.local via the automatic local SSO provider.

Safety rule

Inspect freely. Do not approve, revoke, rotate, offboard, deploy, or dispatch unless this guide marks it optional.

Use two browser tabs

Right-click Open the trstctl demo and choose “Open Link in New Tab.” Leave this walkthrough open so you can move back and forth.

The whole product in one picture

trstctl manages credentials used by machines, not people. A machine might be a web service, build bot, Kubernetes workload, router, database, or AI agent. trstctl finds its credentials, applies policy, records every state change, and delivers replacements.

DiscoverFind certificates, secrets, tokens, SSH keys, and workloads.
DecideApply tenant policy, ownership, risk, and approval rules.
IssueAsk an isolated signer or external CA for a credential.
DeliverUse a connector to place it where the machine needs it.
ProveKeep immutable events, receipts, audit exports, and evidence packs.

Demo numbers can move

Background lifecycle workers keep running. Counts may differ from screenshots or from one refresh to the next. Focus on the names, workflow stages, and evidence—not an exact number.

The guided tour

Complete each stop in order. Check the box when the expected result makes sense.

1

Sign in and identify your tenant

Safe Establish the operator session and storage boundary.

Click

  1. Open demo home
  2. If you see the sign-in page, click Sign in with SSO. The local identity provider signs you in automatically.
  3. Look at the top-right corner for demo-admin@trstctl.local.
  4. Look at the top bar for Tenant and the UUID beginning 11111111.
Expected: You land on Dashboard; the demo administrator and tenant are visible.
Why it matters: The session supplies the tenant. The browser does not get to invent a tenant ID, and PostgreSQL row-level security enforces that boundary below the API.
2

Read the control-plane dashboard

Read only See inventory, alerts, expiry, issuance, and activity together.

Click and inspect

  1. Click Dashboard in the left navigation.
  2. Notice the left rail has three parts: a Module switcher (Certificates & PKI, Secrets, SSH, Signing, Fleet) that scopes the middle band to one product; the always-present global bands (Inventory, Detect & respond, Govern & administer) for cross-product surfaces like Identities, Credential risk, Credential graph, and Audit; and the Needs action worklists. Switching modules changes only the scoped band—the global bands never move.
  3. Read the four top counters: Certificates, Identities (NHI), Secrets, and Agents online. The action tiles below (Expiring ≤7d, High-risk, Open incidents, Future-ready) are wired to live tenant data.
  4. In Non-human identity inventory, compare certificates, tokens, and API keys. One control plane models several credential types.
  5. Scroll through Alert center, Issuance rate, Renewal jobs, Expiration timeline, and Algorithm mix.
  6. Find Rotate first and Recent activity. These connect posture to the next operator action and its audit evidence.
Expected: A populated tenant overview, including expiring demo certificates and algorithm distribution.
Capability: Unified inventory and lifecycle telemetry across X.509, machine identities, secrets, tokens, and agents.

Use Journeys as the product map

Safe See complete operator outcomes instead of disconnected features.

  1. Click Journeys in the left navigation, or open it directly.

Capability: End-to-end outcomes that chain discovery, policy, issuance, delivery, revocation, and evidence.

Find unmanaged credentials with Discovery

Read only Turn unknown credentials into a triage queue.

  1. Under Detect & respond, click Discovery.
  2. Read the Shadow inventory counters: findings, high risk, and finding types.
  3. On the Findings tab, inspect the demo findings. A finding is something trstctl observed but does not yet fully manage.
  4. Open Sources. Look for network, manual, CT-log, cloud certificate, cloud secret, and drift sources.
  5. Open Schedules and Runs to see how repeatable scans are represented.
Expected: A populated shadow-inventory summary and several configured discovery-source types.

5

Inspect certificate inventory and estate health

Read only Understand what exists, who owns it, and when it expires.

  1. In the Module switcher select Certificates & PKI, then click Certificates.
  2. Stay on Inventory. Use the search box to search for demo.trstctl.local.
  3. Open one row to inspect subject, issuer, owner, fingerprint, algorithm, validity, source, and deployment location.
  4. Click Estate health to view expiry and inventory posture across the tenant.
  5. Click CRL & CT to see revocation and certificate-transparency evidence.
  6. Click Renewal readiness to distinguish “expires soon” from “can be renewed automatically.”
  7. Notice the links to CA hierarchy and Certificate profiles. CAs sign; profiles restrict what may be signed.
Expected: Imported, discovered, and trstctl-issued certificates in one searchable inventory.
Capability: Certificate lifecycle management from inventory through renewal, revocation, and transparency evidence.
6

Follow a non-human identity lifecycle

Read only Separate the machine identity from the credential it currently holds.

  1. Click Identities.
  2. Read the Issuance pipeline: issued, deployed, and revoked are lifecycle states.
  3. Find a demo identity such as payments-api.demo.trstctl.local or iot-est-gateway.demo.trstctl.local.
  4. Click View details. Inspect owner, issuer, status, attributes, and credential relationships.
  5. Return to the list. Notice that Retire and other lifecycle actions are separate from viewing details.
Expected: A lifecycle table with multiple identity states and detailed ownership/issuance metadata.
Capability: Durable NHI lifecycle state. Rotating a certificate does not create an unrelated “new machine”; it advances the identity and records the replacement relationship.
7

See every enabled enrollment protocol

Read only Machines can request credentials using the protocol they already understand.

  1. In the Certificates & PKI module, click Protocols.
  2. Read Protocol responder status and the fail-closed explanation.
  3. Scroll through the register. The evaluation profile activates ACME, EST, SCEP, CMP, SSH, TSA, and SPIFFE.
  4. For ACME, connect “account + challenge + profile” to automated web-service certificates.
  5. For EST/SCEP/CMP, think of devices, MDM, networking gear, and enterprise PKI enrollment.
  6. For SPIFFE, think of short-lived workload identities delivered through a local workload socket.
  7. Open SSH trust and Code signing from the page header to see adjacent credential families.
Expected: Enabled/served responder rows with tenant-bound setup guidance.
Capability: One policy and signer boundary behind several standards. KMIP is intentionally not part of the one-click eval profile because it needs appliance-grade mutual-TLS material.

Explore secrets, tokens, engines, and sync

Read only Manage more than certificates without treating reveal-once values as ordinary text.

  1. In the Module switcher select Secrets, then click Secrets.
  2. On Store, expand the demo folders. Notice familiar path-like organization such as demo/github/actions.
  3. Click Access to see the API-key/token access surface.
  4. Click Sharing. One-time shares expose material once and then expire.
  5. Click Engines for dynamic credentials, PKI, and transit-style cryptographic operations.
  6. Click CI scanning for secret detection in delivery pipelines.
  7. Click Sync to see outbound synchronization targets.
Expected: Stored secret metadata and tabs for access, sharing, engines, scanning, and synchronization.
Capability: Encrypted secret storage, reveal-once credentials, dynamic backends, and outbound sync—all behind the same tenant and audit boundary.
9

Inspect fleet-wide cryptographic posture

Read only Find weak algorithms, drift, and unexpected public certificates.

  1. Under Detect & respond, click Crypto posture.
  2. Read Certificate Transparency monitoring. CT logs reveal publicly issued certificates, including ones trstctl did not request.
  3. Read Drift detection. Drift means the deployed credential or file state no longer matches intent.
  4. Find CBOM and cryptographic observability. CBOM means “cryptographic bill of materials”: algorithms, key sizes, and TLS posture.
  5. Read Crypto-agility readiness. This is the migration view for weak or post-quantum-vulnerable algorithms.
Expected: Separate CT, drift, CBOM, and crypto-agility sections with clear empty states where no live scan has run.
Capability: Fleet posture is broader than certificate expiry. It includes where crypto is used, whether it drifted, and how safely it can be migrated.
10

Prioritize what to rotate first

Read only Convert many findings into an ordered worklist.

  1. Under Detect & respond, click Credential risk.
  2. Read the summary: credentials scored, critical, high, orphaned, and average score.
  3. In Contextual priorities, compare the base score with contextual score. Blast radius can make a moderate credential urgent.
  4. Find the demo entries such as old-vpn.demo.trstctl.local:443 and ci/buildkite/release-token.
  5. Review NHI policy compliance, over-privilege, stale and dormant NHIs, static credentials, and exposed deployments.
  6. Open Risk bands to see how scores map to critical, high, medium, low, and none.
Expected: A ranked list with human-readable reasons such as blast radius, orphaned owner, near expiry, and stale rotation.
Capability: Risk is explainable and actionable, not a mysterious score. The operator can see exactly why one credential ranks above another.

Explore credential blast radius

  1. Click Credential graph.
  2. Read the node and edge counts. Nodes are identities, credentials, owners, issuers, or resources; edges are relationships.
  3. In Blast radius explorer, choose any populated credential.
  4. Inspect the map and analysis rail. Use Zoom in, Zoom out, and Reset view.

  6. Open Advanced query to see the read-only graph-query surface.

Capability: Incident scope and rotation priority come from relationships, not only from a flat certificate row.

See governance and immutable evidence

Read only Understand approval, policy, and audit as one chain.

  1. Under Detect & respond, click Approvals.
  2. Inspect pending issue/rotate/revoke decisions. Do not click an approve button during the read-only tour.
  3. Click an Audit trail link for a request.
  4. Next click Policy. Read Enforcement path: the backend evaluates policy before it emits the state-change event.
  5. Inspect Policy versions and the compliance framework buttons. Select SOC 2, FIPS 140, or another framework.
  6. Click Audit. Read Hash-chain status, apply a type filter if desired, and click View event on one row.
  7. Notice Export evidence. Audit/export remains part of the auditable core.
Expected: Pending decisions, policy controls/evidence packs, and a long immutable event list with hashes.
Capability: Dual control prevents one actor from silently performing sensitive operations; policy decisions and resulting events remain attributable and exportable.
13

Preview credential-compromise response

Safe preview See replacement-before-revocation and response automation.

  1. Click Incidents.
  2. Read Credential compromise execution. The intended order is: scope, issue replacement, deploy replacement, then revoke the old credential.
  3. Select an affected identity if the field is populated, then click Preview blast radius. Do not click Execute incident.
  4. Review Automated remediation playbooks: revoke, rotate, and right-size.
  5. Read SIEM / SOAR / ITSM dispatch. External actions are queued through the event/outbox path instead of being called inline.
  6. Scroll to Execution evidence and Remediation evidence.
Expected: A previewable compromise workflow, remediation playbooks, external-response forms, and evidence sections.
Capability: Safe incident choreography avoids revoking first and causing an outage. External systems receive durable, retryable outbox work.

Inspect connector delivery and receipts

Read only See how credentials leave the control plane safely.

  1. Under Govern & administer, click Deployment connectors.
  2. Read the four concepts: target setup, identity binding, action, and receipt evidence.
  3. Scroll through Connector registry. The shipped registry covers infrastructure targets such as web servers, proxies, clouds, Kubernetes, and secret platforms.
  4. Inspect Recent delivery receipts. A failed demo receipt is still useful evidence: it proves an attempted external delivery and its error path.
  5. Inspect Outbox circuit breakers. An open circuit pauses a broken destination so it cannot exhaust the whole control plane.
  6. Do not create a target or click Deploy without real target configuration.
Expected: A served connector registry, delivery receipts, and per-destination circuit-breaker state.
Capability: Connectors are runtime-wired delivery implementations. Bounded queues, receipts, retries, rollback metadata, and circuit breakers make external delivery operable.

  3. Click the System posture tab, then the Editions & license tab. Licensing and commercial-edition rows now live on their own tab, separate from operational access and system posture.

Optional hands-on lab

These actions change demo state. They are safe only because this is a disposable local environment.

Stop here if you want the original seed unchanged

The main tour is complete. The exercises below create records. Do not use them against a real deployment unless you understand the policy and operational impact.

Exercise A — create a harmless owner record
  1. Open Owners under Inventory.
  2. Choose the create-owner action.
  3. Use kind team, name Walkthrough Team, and an obviously fake local email such as walkthrough@example.invalid.
  4. Submit once. If the UI asks for an idempotency key, keep the generated value rather than resubmitting with a different one.
  5. Open Audit and search for the owner event.

Lesson: A mutation creates tenant-scoped state and immutable evidence.

Exercise B — inspect, but do not approve, a governed rotation
  1. Open Approvals.
  2. Select a pending rotate/revoke row and open its Audit trail.
  3. Identify requester, requested action, target identity, and required approval posture.
  4. Return without clicking Approve.

Lesson: Enterprise governance deliberately converts a sensitive direct action into a pending dual-control decision.

Exercise C — export evidence
  1. Open Audit.
  2. Apply a narrow event-type or time filter.
  3. Click Export evidence.
  4. Keep the export only as demo data; do not confuse it with a certification.

Lesson: trstctl can hand an auditor signed, tamper-evident product evidence while clearly separating operator attestations and external certification residuals.

Plain-language glossary

The shortest useful definition of the terms used in the UI.

NHI
Non-human identity: a service, workload, bot, device, agent, or other machine actor.
Credential
Proof a machine uses to authenticate, such as a certificate, secret, API key, token, SSH certificate, or SVID.
Identity
The durable machine record. It can hold different credentials over time as they rotate.
X.509 certificate
A signed digital identity document used mainly for TLS and device/workload authentication.
CA
Certificate authority: the system trusted to sign certificates.
Profile
Rules limiting what a CA may issue: lifetime, names, algorithms, purposes, and protocol.
Tenant
An isolated customer or organizational boundary. Every stored row and query is tenant-scoped.
RLS
PostgreSQL row-level security: the database itself prevents one tenant from reading another tenant’s rows.
Event
An immutable statement that something happened. Events are the source of truth for lifecycle state.
Projection
A query-friendly table built from events, such as the certificate inventory or audit view.
Outbox
A durable queue record written with the state change before an external call is attempted.
Connector
A runtime implementation that deploys, tests, rolls back, or right-sizes a credential at a target system.
Signer
The isolated process that performs private-key operations. It is separate from the web control plane.
Rotation
Issue a replacement credential, deploy it, verify it, and retire/revoke the predecessor.
Blast radius
The identities and resources affected if a credential is stolen, revoked, or unavailable.
CBOM
Cryptographic bill of materials: an inventory of algorithms, key sizes, TLS versions, and crypto dependencies.
SPIFFE / SVID
A workload-identity standard and its short-lived identity document.
FIPS
A regulated cryptographic-module posture. A license flag alone does not turn a normal binary into a FIPS-built artifact.

Troubleshooting

Simple recovery steps for the local demo.

The page says “token exchange failed”

Close the error tab, reopen the demo root, and click Sign in with SSO again. If it repeats, the local OIDC sidecar needs to be reattached; ask the demo operator to restart it.

The browser cannot connect

Confirm the address is exactly http://127.0.0.1:18081—not HTTPS, not port 9443, and not a remote hostname. The demo stack must remain running on this Mac.

A screen has no rows

Read the empty-state explanation. Some integrations need a real external target or scan input. “No rows” is different from “not implemented”; the page should say what configuration or action is missing.

A connector receipt says failed

That is expected when the demo has no real vendor endpoint or credentials. The useful part is the durable attempt, error, retry/circuit-breaker state, and evidence receipt.

The FIPS row says licensed but inactive

Correct. The demo uses the standard image. Actual approved-mode testing requires the artifact produced by make fips-build plus the deployment-specific validation and operator artifacts listed on the Platform page.
